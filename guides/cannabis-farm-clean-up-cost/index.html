What pushes the price up

  • Number of rooms used and whether the loft was used
  • Electrical tampering: a bypassed meter almost always means a full test, and often a rewire
  • How long the grow ran: longer means more damp, mould and plaster damage
  • Structural alterations: holes in ceilings, walls and floors, removed doors
  • Access: flats above shops, city-centre parking and skip permits
  • Waste volume and type: electrical items and chemicals cost more to dispose of properly

A real Birmingham bill

Birmingham Live reported the case of a family house on Newcroft Grove in Yardley, let to a couple who used it to grow cannabis. Police found the farm after neighbours raised the alarm, and the tenants fled. The family said they paid nearly £5,000 in total, which they could not claim on insurance, and that this included nearly £3,000 to the network operator (then Western Power, now National Grid Electricity Distribution) to dig up soil and reconnect the electricity. The rest went on repairing fixtures and fittings, repainting, cleaning and removing rubbish.

Two lessons from that case:

  • The supply side can cost as much as the clean-up. If the cable was tapped before the meter, the network operator's repair is a cost of its own.
  • Uninsured is common. Check your cover before you need it.

At the other end of the scale

LandlordZone reported a landlord facing a £60,000 repair bill after three of his properties were turned into cannabis farms. That is around £20,000 a property, at the top of the published restoration ranges above. Large grows, long-running grows and structural alterations push costs to that level.

Lost rent is a cost too

The published figures above do not include the rent you lose while the property is empty. To work out your own figure, multiply the monthly rent by the months from the police visit to the first rent from a new tenant. As an illustration only: a house let at £900 a month that takes three months to restore and re-let has lost £2,700 of rent on top of the repair bill. Some landlord policies include loss of rent cover after an insured event. Check yours.

Who pays for the stolen electricity?

Energy suppliers can try to recover the cost of stolen electricity. Who ends up liable depends on the facts, the supply contract and who was the customer at the address. If you are billed, ask the supplier for the basis of the charge in writing and take advice. Stay Energy Safe, run by Crimestoppers, explains the dangers and how to report theft.

VAT and how to compare quotes

Some published prices are plus VAT, some are not. Ask every firm to quote the same way, and compare like with like:

Ask for Why
A room-by-room scope So you can see what is included and what is not
Separate lines for waste, cleaning, electrics, damp, repairs Insurers and adjudicators assess each item
Their waste carrier registration number So you can check it before they start
What happens if they find more Hidden damage is common; agree a day rate or a variation process
Payment terms Avoid large sums up front
What paperwork you get at the end Photos, waste transfer notes, EICR, invoices

Ballpark by size, from the published figures

Putting the sources on this page together, and only as a rough guide:

  • Small grow, one or two rooms, no structural work: clean-up in the region of £900 to £1,500, plus an electrician's make-safe and test, plus decoration.
  • Medium grow, several rooms and the loft: clean-up from around £2,000, very likely electrical remedial work, plus damp treatment and repairs. Total bills in the published ranges of roughly £10,000 to £26,000 are realistic.
  • Large or long-running grow, structural alterations: the top of the published ranges and beyond, as in the £60,000 three-property case.

These bands come from the published sources above, not from our own jobs. A survey and written quote are the only real price.

Costs people forget

  • Locks. In the mydeposits case, a full lock change cost £220.
  • Boarding and securing while the property is empty.
  • Supply-side repairs by the network operator, as in the Yardley case.
  • Skip permits if a skip has to sit on the road.
  • A new EICR after electrical remedial work, plus a new gas safety record if the boiler or pipework was touched.
  • Licence costs if a licensed property needs a variation or a new application.
  • Your own time, and agent fees for extra visits and re-letting.

Put each one on your list early so the final total does not come as a shock, and so your insurer sees the full claim at the start.

Questions landlords ask

Is cannabis farm clean-up cheaper if I do it myself?

The labour is cheaper, but you still need a registered waste carrier for disposal, a registered electrician for the make-safe and test, and evidence for any claim. Mould, sharp fixings and live cables make it risky.

Does landlord insurance cover the clean-up?

Some policies do, some exclude it, and many attach conditions such as regular inspections and tenant referencing. The Yardley family in the case above said their costs could not be claimed. Read your wording.

Why do quotes vary so much?

Firms price different things. One may quote waste only; another may include cleaning, odour treatment and a report. Ask for room-by-room, itemised quotes so you can compare like with like.
