Will landlord insurance pay for cannabis farm damage? The inspection trap

Some landlord policies do cover damage from a tenant growing drugs, but the cover often comes with conditions. A published Financial Ombudsman Service decision shows exactly how a claim can fail on those conditions, even when the damage is obvious.

The case: DRN-6210910

This is a published Financial Ombudsman Service final decision. The landlord, Mr S, complained that Great Lakes Insurance SE unfairly declined his landlord insurance claim.

What happened. In November 2024, after getting a possession order, Mr S found his property had been used to grow cannabis. He claimed for the damage and three months of unpaid rent.

What the policy said. The cover for damage from a tenant's cultivation of drugs applied provided that the landlord, or someone acting for them:

  • carried out internal and external inspections at least every three months, or as permitted under the tenancy agreement
  • kept a log of those inspections for at least 24 months
  • carried out a six-monthly management check of that log

What the records showed. Documented inspections took place in January 2023, May 2023 and December 2023, with none in 2024. The gaps were roughly four, seven and six months. The landlord said he had also made casual quarterly visits, but there was little evidence of them. A March 2024 record said only "no access" and "no response".

What the ombudsman decided. The ombudsman found the insurer's reading of the three-month requirement reasonable, found that the requirement had been breached, and accepted that the breach was material: regular inspections are meant to deter tenants from setting up a grow, and longer gaps make it easier. The condition had been highlighted in the policy summary under significant exclusions and limitations. The complaint was not upheld, so the claim stayed declined.

Why this matters to every landlord

  • Six-monthly inspections are common practice, but some policies require three-monthly. The ombudsman said it is the policy terms that count, not general practice.
  • "No access" is not an inspection. Repeated refusals are a warning sign. Record them, follow them up in writing, and take advice.
  • A log is a condition, not a nice-to-have. If the policy requires a log, an undocumented visit may not count.

What to do now, before anything goes wrong

  1. Read your policy wording for malicious damage, illegal cultivation of drugs, and unoccupancy. Note any inspection frequency and record-keeping requirements.
  2. Check your tenancy agreement allows access at that frequency with proper notice.
  3. Put inspections in the diary at the required interval and keep a written log with dates, who attended, what was checked, and photos.
  4. Review the log at whatever interval the policy requires.
  5. If you use a letting agent, check their management agreement covers inspections at your policy's frequency and that you get copies of the reports.

If you have already found a cannabis farm

  • Report the claim promptly and in writing.
  • Give the insurer your inspection records. If there are gaps, explain them honestly and provide evidence of attempts to get access.
  • If the claim is declined and you disagree, use the insurer's complaints process first. If you are still unhappy after eight weeks, or after a final response, you can usually take it to the Financial Ombudsman Service, which is free.
  • Get the property made safe and documented either way. See the first 48 hours checklist.

A second decision: referencing as well as inspections

Another published ombudsman decision, DRN-5319905, concerned Royal & Sun Alliance (RSA) and a commercial property policy covering a rented property. The policy covered damage from the illegal cultivation of drugs, but only if the landlord had:

  • carried out internal and external inspections at least every three months and kept a log for at least 24 months
  • obtained, verified and kept written references from an employer, guarantor or former landlord before the tenant moved in
  • obtained and kept photo ID such as a passport or driving licence
  • recorded the tenant's bank account details and verified them by receiving at least one payment from that account

The landlord had not inspected for around five months before the damage was found and had not obtained references before the tenancy started. He argued the grow could have been set up in a day and would have happened anyway. The ombudsman did not accept that the breach made no difference and did not uphold the complaint. RSA's offer of £100 for customer service failings stood, but the claim stayed declined.

The conditions are similar across insurers

Allianz has published an example of its own wording for drug cultivation damage. It requires internal and external inspections at least every six months, a log kept for at least 24 months, formal identification of the tenant, and the tenant's bank details verified by at least one payment. Allianz says that when it validates these claims, the tenants have often given false references.

So one insurer may say three months and another six. Your wording is the only one that matters. Find the clause, write the interval in your diary and keep the evidence.

A simple inspection log

You do not need software. A spreadsheet or a notebook works if every entry has:

Field Example
Date and time 14 March 2026, 10:30
Who attended Landlord and agent
Notice given Written notice on 11 March
Rooms entered All, including loft hatch opened
What you saw No issues. Bathroom extractor not working
Photos taken 12, stored in folder "2026-03 inspection"
Action Extractor repair booked

If you could not get in, record that as well, with the dates you tried and copies of the letters or messages. One "no access" is a warning; two in a row is a reason to take advice.

Referencing checks to keep on file

The decisions above and the Crimestoppers guidance point to the same checks:

  • Photo ID, copied and kept
  • References from an employer or previous landlord that you actually verified, not just received
  • Rent paid from a UK bank account in the tenant's name, with at least one payment received from it
  • A note of anything unusual: an offer to pay months in advance in cash, a request for no inspections, a wish to keep the bills in your name. Crimestoppers lists all of these as warning signs. See our warning signs guide.

How to make the claim

  1. Report it promptly, by phone and then in writing, quoting the crime reference.
  2. Send your inspection log and referencing file with the first letter, not when asked.
  3. Ask in writing whether the insurer wants to inspect before work starts, and whether you can appoint your own contractors.
  4. Get itemised quotes. Clearance, electrics, damp, each repair and decoration as separate lines.
  5. Ask about loss of rent cover and what evidence is needed for it.
  6. Keep a timeline of every call: date, who you spoke to, what was said.

If the claim is declined

Complain to the insurer first, in writing, and ask for a final response. If you have not had one within eight weeks, or you disagree with it, you can usually take the complaint to the Financial Ombudsman Service. It is free for consumers and for many small businesses. As the two decisions show, the ombudsman looks at whether the condition was clear and whether the breach was relevant, so evidence of the checks you did make matters.

Questions landlords ask

Do all landlord policies cover cannabis farm damage?

No. Some exclude it, some include it as standard and some offer it as an extra. Where it is covered, conditions such as inspections and tenant referencing are common. Read the wording, not just the summary.

My agent does inspections every six months. Is that enough?

It depends entirely on your policy. In the case above, the policy required three-monthly inspections and six-monthly was not enough.

My policy says inspections 'as permitted under the tenancy agreement'. What does that mean?

It usually means you must inspect as often as your tenancy agreement allows, with proper notice. Check both documents together and make sure the tenancy gives you access at least as often as the policy requires.
