Signs of a cannabis farm in a rental property

Most landlords who find a cannabis farm say afterwards that there were signs. Here are the ones Crimestoppers lists, the tenant behaviour that should worry you at referencing, and what to do if you suspect something, without putting yourself at risk.

Why it matters to you

Cannabis farms in rented houses are not a small-time hobby. Crimestoppers says organised crime gangs rent properties in towns and the countryside to grow cannabis, and that the farms bring serious violence, victims of modern slavery and a higher risk of fire. For a landlord, the practical risks are a house that is badly damaged, an insurance claim that may fail, and lost rent while it is put right. Spotting the signs early limits all three.

Signs at the property

Crimestoppers lists these signs that a property is being used to grow cannabis:

  • A powerful, distinctive, sweet and sickly smell
  • Frequent visitors throughout the day and night
  • Blacked-out windows, or vents sealed or blocked to stop heat and smell escaping
  • Chinks of bright light throughout the night
  • Birds gathering on the roof, especially in cold weather
  • Snow melting unusually quickly on the roof in winter
  • Heavy condensation on the windows
  • Noise from fans
  • Large amounts of rubbish, including compost bags
  • The electricity meter tampered with, or new cabling, sometimes leading to street lighting

None of these proves anything on its own. Condensation is common in winter, and plenty of people have visitors. It is the combination that matters, and how it changes over time.

Signs at referencing and during the tenancy

Crimestoppers also lists warning signs for landlords and letting agents about the tenant:

  • Poor or no references from a previous letting agent
  • Offering to pay several months in advance
  • Wanting to pay rent in cash without a good reason
  • Wanting to keep the utility bills in the landlord's name
  • Asking for complete privacy, asking for no periodic inspections, or preventing inspections even when given proper notice
  • Changing the locks to keep the landlord or agent out

It adds that growers may bar entry to maintenance workers or limit where they can go.

An offer of six months' rent up front can look like a good tenant. It can also be a way of making sure nobody visits for six months.

What your insurer expects you to have done

Many landlord policies that cover drug cultivation damage have conditions linked to exactly these signs: regular internal and external inspections with a log, photo ID, verified references, and rent paid from a verified bank account. Two published Financial Ombudsman decisions show claims failing because those conditions were not met. Our insurance conditions guide explains both.

What to do if you are suspicious

Do not investigate on your own. Crimestoppers warns that rival gangs break into grow houses to steal crops and equipment, and that violence can break out. Electrical tampering can also cause electrocution or fire.

  1. Write down what you have seen, with dates and times. Photos from the street are fine; do not trespass.
  2. Report it. Call West Midlands Police on 101, or 999 if you think a crime is happening now. If you would rather stay anonymous, call Crimestoppers on 0800 555 111.
  3. If the meter or cabling looks tampered with, do not touch it. Report it to 105 or anonymously to Stay Energy Safe.
  4. Tell your insurer if your policy asks you to report a change in risk.
  5. Keep up with lawful inspections. Give proper written notice under the tenancy agreement. If access is refused, record it and take advice.

Do not enter without notice, change the locks or cut off utilities to force the issue. That can be a criminal offence against the tenant, even if you turn out to be right.

If you go in and find one

If you or your agent enter lawfully, for example at a booked inspection, and find plants growing, leave straight away. Do not touch any equipment or wiring. Call the police and give them the address and what you saw. Then follow our first 48 hours guide.

Prevention that actually helps

  • Reference properly. Verify references by phone, not just email, and check photo ID against the person in front of you.
  • Take rent by bank transfer from an account in the tenant's name, and note the account details.
  • Inspect at the interval your insurer requires, not just at the interval that is usual. Keep a log with photos.
  • Visit the outside between inspections. Windows, bins and the meter box tell you a lot.
  • Be wary of unusual requests, such as keeping bills in your name, paying months ahead in cash, or "no visits please".
  • If you use an agent, check the management agreement covers inspections at your policy's frequency and that you get the reports.

Commercial and empty properties

Crimestoppers notes that farms are found in vacant shops, industrial units, warehouses, empty pubs and disused factories, as well as homes. Walsall's Midland Road factory, with more than 1,700 plants, is a local example covered on our Walsall page. If you own an empty unit, check your insurer's unoccupancy conditions and visit it regularly. A unit nobody visits is exactly what growers look for.

What you may see inside at an inspection

Crimestoppers says rented homes and commercial units used for growing are usually altered with barricaded windows and doors, unsafe wiring, and mould and water damage from irrigation. At a booked inspection, be alert to:

  • Rooms you are told you cannot enter, or doors that have been fitted with new locks
  • Foil, plastic sheeting or staples on walls and ceilings
  • Flexible ducting, or holes cut into ceilings or the loft hatch
  • Extension leads and cables running to rooms that should not need them
  • Water staining on ceilings below bedrooms
  • A strong smell masked by air fresheners

If you see these, do not open locked doors or touch equipment. Finish the visit calmly, leave, and call the police.

For letting agents

Agents often see the early signs first, because they handle the viewings, the rent and the inspections. If something does not add up, tell the landlord in writing and record it. Our letting agents page explains how we work with agents after a find.

Questions landlords ask

Is a high electricity bill a sign of a cannabis farm?

It can be, but growers often bypass the meter, so the recorded use can look normal or even low. A tampered meter or new cabling is a stronger sign.

Can I enter if I think there is a cannabis farm?

Not without proper notice or another lawful reason. If you think a crime is happening, call the police and let them deal with it. Entering or changing locks without a lawful basis can be an offence.

Should I refuse a tenant who offers rent up front?

Not automatically, but treat it as a reason to check references, ID and the source of the money carefully. Crimestoppers lists it as a warning sign.
