f:Tf70,<h2>The case: DRN-6210910</h2>
<p>This is a published Financial Ombudsman Service final decision. The landlord, Mr S, complained that <strong>Great Lakes Insurance SE</strong> unfairly declined his landlord insurance claim.</p>
<p><strong>What happened.</strong> In November 2024, after getting a possession order, Mr S found his property had been used to grow cannabis. He claimed for the damage and three months of unpaid rent.</p>
<p><strong>What the policy said.</strong> The cover for damage from a tenant&#39;s cultivation of drugs applied provided that the landlord, or someone acting for them:</p>
<ul>
<li>carried out <strong>internal and external inspections at least every three months</strong>, or as permitted under the tenancy agreement</li>
<li><strong>kept a log</strong> of those inspections for at least 24 months</li>
<li>carried out a <strong>six-monthly management check</strong> of that log</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>What the records showed.</strong> Documented inspections took place in January 2023, May 2023 and December 2023, with none in 2024. The gaps were roughly four, seven and six months. The landlord said he had also made casual quarterly visits, but there was little evidence of them. A March 2024 record said only &quot;no access&quot; and &quot;no response&quot;.</p>
<p><strong>What the ombudsman decided.</strong> The ombudsman found the insurer&#39;s reading of the three-month requirement reasonable, found that the requirement had been breached, and accepted that the breach was material: regular inspections are meant to deter tenants from setting up a grow, and longer gaps make it easier. The condition had been highlighted in the policy summary under significant exclusions and limitations. The complaint was <strong>not upheld</strong>, so the claim stayed declined.</p>
<h2>Why this matters to every landlord</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Six-monthly inspections are common practice, but some policies require three-monthly.</strong> The ombudsman said it is the policy terms that count, not general practice.</li>
<li><strong>&quot;No access&quot; is not an inspection.</strong> Repeated refusals are a warning sign. Record them, follow them up in writing, and take advice.</li>
<li><strong>A log is a condition, not a nice-to-have.</strong> If the policy requires a log, an undocumented visit may not count.</li>
</ul>
<h2>What to do now, before anything goes wrong</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Read your policy wording</strong> for malicious damage, illegal cultivation of drugs, and unoccupancy. Note any inspection frequency and record-keeping requirements.</li>
<li><strong>Check your tenancy agreement</strong> allows access at that frequency with proper notice.</li>
<li><strong>Put inspections in the diary</strong> at the required interval and keep a written log with dates, who attended, what was checked, and photos.</li>
<li><strong>Review the log</strong> at whatever interval the policy requires.</li>
<li><strong>If you use a letting agent</strong>, check their management agreement covers inspections at your policy&#39;s frequency and that you get copies of the reports.</li>
</ol>
<h2>If you have already found a cannabis farm</h2>
<ul>
<li>Report the claim promptly and in writing.</li>
<li>Give the insurer your inspection records. If there are gaps, explain them honestly and provide evidence of attempts to get access.</li>
<li>If the claim is declined and you disagree, use the insurer&#39;s complaints process first. If you are still unhappy after eight weeks, or after a final response, you can usually take it to the Financial Ombudsman Service, which is free.</li>
<li>Get the property made safe and documented either way. See the <a href="/mercia-property-recovery/guides/first-48-hours-after-a-cannabis-farm/">first 48 hours checklist</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>This page summarises a published decision for general information. It is not legal or insurance advice, and every policy is different.</p>
